Dream-Work and "Isle of Dreams": A Cross-Reading of Lyotard and Hino

2 March 2026

A

When Jean-François Lyotard maps out that "From the read to the seen, we pass from a 'horizontal', flat, atopic negativity, to a 'vertical', deep, place-making negativity" (30) in "The Dream-Work Does Not Think," I imagine a category of video essays on YouTube (those by Hoog are good examples) in which two-dimensional visuals begin to blossom with embellishments that look three-dimensional. The two-dimensional 'substratum' is largely monochrome; by contrast, the blooms of 3-D are garishly colorful, signposting the information that we should really pay attention to, whatever the content of that information. Think about the name of a chromosome, which describes nothing about what is in one (the genes, structural elements, etc.) but instead points vaguely to some mysterious object X that is dyed under a microscope.


Earlier, in tracking the path textual fragments take before landing in their places within the text (am I giving it away by using the verb 'land'?), Lyotard answers how "the extract move[s] above the text" but then says that "This movement takes place, therefore, in depth" (7). The choice of 'depth' to describe the distance of the extract carries a different connotation than, let's say, 'altitude', which would be more apt if "above the text" referred to air travel. Depth introduces a below-the-ground feeling which seems to undermine the bird's-eye-view Lyotard momentarily granted his extracts as they each migrated to reassemble their spots. This aboveness, though, a tryst played against the vertigo of the surface below, is a visualization Lyotard cannot yet abandon: the passage "from the energistic to the linguistic," which in his eyes gets our dream to look like it says something to us, is a "selective power directing these upheavals to deposit their products in a readable manner" (30, emphasis added). It may be to further drive a problematic into Lacan's strong theory [see below] of the signifier [S'] covering the signified [S] from above (which he already does when he writes that in Saussure's original version of the formula, the bar between them is "far from representing repression or censorship" (19) and before any of that puts S' below S) that Lyotard refuses to decide whether the seen falls onto the read from above or carves out a canyon system below the read.


It is said that the human eye initially captures the world 'upside down' but that our brain 'corrects' for the supposed inaccuracy by flipping our vision back. If 'up' and 'down' can be designated arbitrarily, what exactly is the correctiveness of this correction? (Which miscreant flipped our vision 'upside down' in the first place?) In the same way, why do we associate the phrase 'above the text' and the word 'deposit' with an absolutely unidirectional gravitational force? Could fragments not sediment from below, or fly below the surface while travelling above the text? Literary criticism is fascinated with the palimpsest, whose relevance rests on the presumption that whatever is on the topmost layer of paper is the most visible. What if had once been decided instead that the bottommost layer of paper would have the strongest legible traces of ink? My point here is to think with Lyotard about the material reality of the metaphors that are used to emboss our slippery communications toward the unconscious or toward deep history with something more intuitable in, well, material reality.

泡のように増え続け あてもない人の洪水 不思議な裏の世界 私はさよならする


[They're duplicating like bubbles / A flood of people going nowhere / Mystiqueful underworld / I bid farewells]

—Ōnuki Taeko, "Tokai" (from her album Sunshower, 1977)

What Lyotard is illuminating is, along with the 3-D blooming of dreams themselves, the weird spatiality of the epithet-like association people have whenever the word 'dream' is used as a descriptive tool in the waking world. A dreamlike geography might not be a geography in a dream, so it must be a quality other than being inside a dream that makes something dreamlike. Here, I want to rope in Hino Keizō's Isle of Dreams (夢の島). Throughout the whole of this novel—which tracks a fifty-something salaryman named Sakai (the Kanji of this surname, 境, fittingly means 'border'—consider: is this a novel of going past borders or borders dissolving?) slowly beginning to see the apparitional glow of the uniform metropolis and, as a consequence, becoming drawn to its empty peripheries (the bare reclaimed land pre-development, the churning landfills, and eventually, an 'island' in Tokyo Bay that is part jungle, part abandoned watchtower, and part ecological disaster) and characters he perceives as subaltern (a woman on a motorbike, another woman who makes art pieces with mannequins, a child who lives on the streets)—there is not a single explicit pointer that the action takes place inside anybody's dream. Why, then, is it possible to argue that the environments the writing lives in are dreamlike? If we are, as I would like, to contend that this dreamlikeness is generated in the way we are led to imagine space, how does the text lead us that route? And finally, is aestheticization a way to dismiss voices (in Hino's case, especially women's voices) by dissolving them into the beautiful imagery?
