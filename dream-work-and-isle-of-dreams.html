Dream-Work and "Isle of Dreams": A Cross-Reading of Jean-François Lyotard and Hino Keizō

2 March 2026

A

When Jean-François Lyotard maps out that "From the read to the seen, we pass from a 'horizontal', flat, atopic negativity, to a 'vertical', deep, place-making negativity"[1] in "The Dream-Work Does Not Think," I imagine a category of video essays on YouTube (those by Hoog are good examples) in which two-dimensional visuals begin to blossom with embellishments that look three-dimensional. The two-dimensional 'substratum' is largely monochrome; by contrast, the blooms of 3-D are garishly colorful, signposting the information that we should really pay attention to, whatever the content of that information. Think about the name of a chromosome, which describes nothing about what is in one (the genes, structural elements, etc.) but instead points vaguely to some mysterious object X that is dyed under a microscope.


Earlier, in tracking the path textual fragments take before landing in their places within the text (am I giving it away by using the verb 'land'?), Lyotard answers how "the extract move[s] above the text" but then says that "This movement takes place, therefore, in depth."[2] The choice of 'depth' to describe the distance of the extract carries a different connotation than, let's say, 'altitude', which would be more apt if "above the text" referred to air travel. Depth introduces a below-the-ground feeling which seems to undermine the bird's-eye-view Lyotard momentarily granted his extracts as they each migrated to reassemble their spots. This aboveness, though, a tryst played against the vertigo of the surface below, is a visualization Lyotard cannot yet abandon: the passage "from the energistic to the linguistic," which in his eyes gets our dream to look like it says something to us, is a "selective power directing these upheavals to deposit their products in a readable manner."[3] It may be to further drive a problematic into Lacan's strong theory (see below) of the signifier [S'] covering the signified [S] from above (which he already does when he writes that in Saussure's original version of the formula, the bar between them is "far from representing repression or censorship"[4] and before any of that puts S' below S) that Lyotard refuses to decide whether the seen falls onto the read from above or carves out a canyon system below the read.


It is said that the human eye initially captures the world 'upside down' but that our brain 'corrects' for the supposed inaccuracy by flipping our vision back. If 'up' and 'down' can be designated arbitrarily, what exactly is the correctiveness of this correction? (Which miscreant flipped our vision 'upside down' in the first place?) In the same way, why do we associate the phrase 'above the text' and the word 'deposit' with an absolutely unidirectional gravitational force? Could fragments not sediment from below, or fly below the surface while travelling above the text? Literary criticism is fascinated with the palimpsest, whose relevance rests on the presumption that whatever is on the topmost layer of paper is the most visible. What if had once been decided instead that the bottommost layer of paper would have the strongest legible traces of ink? My point here is to think with Lyotard about the material reality of the metaphors that are used to emboss our slippery communications toward the unconscious or toward deep history with something more intuitable in, well, material reality.


B

泡のように増え続け あてもない人の洪水 不思議な裏の世界 私はさよならする

[They're duplicating like bubbles / A flood of people going nowhere / Mystiqueful underworld / I bid farewells]

—Ōnuki Taeko, "Tokai" (from her album Sunshower, 1977)


What Lyotard is illuminating is, along with the 3-D blooming of dreams themselves, the weird spatiality of the epithet-like association people have whenever the word 'dream' is used as a descriptive tool in the waking world. A dreamlike geography might not be a geography in a dream, so it must be a quality other than being inside a dream that makes something dreamlike. Here, I want to rope in Hino Keizō's Isle of Dreams (夢の島). Throughout the whole of this novel—which tracks a fifty-something salaryman named Sakai (the Kanji of this surname, 境, fittingly means 'border'—consider: is this a novel of going past borders or borders dissolving?) slowly beginning to see the apparitional glow of the uniform metropolis and, as a consequence, becoming drawn to its empty peripheries (the bare reclaimed land pre-development, the churning landfills, and eventually, an 'island' in Tokyo Bay that is part jungle, part abandoned watchtower, and part ecological disaster) and characters he perceives as subaltern (a woman on a motorbike, another woman who makes art pieces with mannequins, a child who lives on the streets)—there is not a single explicit pointer that the action takes place inside anybody's dream. Why, then, is it possible to argue that the environments the writing lives in are dreamlike? If we are, as I would like, to contend that this dreamlikeness is generated in the way we are led to imagine space, how does the text lead us that route? And finally, is aestheticization a way to dismiss voices (in Hino's case, especially women's voices) by dissolving them into the beautiful imagery?


Contention 1: Specifically, we are led to imagine a cognition—is it ours?—growing spatially.

マネキンというのは人間になろうとしてなりきれなかった物質のふしぎな幻像なのか、それとも形だけを残して人間でなくなりかけている奇妙な半生物なのだろうか。

[Could a mannequin be the phantom of matter which wanted to be a human but didn't quite make it, or could it be a half-organism which hangs onto only its shape having almost lost its entire humanness…] [5]


Contention 2: The cognition grows from space and is met halfway by its complement, which had itself been emerging in this direction from the afterimage of a dream.

夢をみることが多くなったのも新しいことだ。これは年齢のせいで夜明け方にふっと目が覚めることが多くなったためもあるが、そういうときベッドを出て居間のソファーに坐っていると、窓ガラス越しに見える下の道路で街灯が二度三度瞬いてから次々にふっと消えてゆく。もう夜ではないがまだ朝でもない微妙な間の薄明の中で、たったいまみた夢の幾つもの場景がビデオカセットをゆっくり巻き戻すように現れてくる。

[It's a new thing for him, seeing this many dreams. He has, it must be admitted, been waking more frequently at dawn due to his age, but also, after he climbs out of bed and sinks into the living room sofa, he sees the streetlights on the road below his window flicker two, three times, then go back to dark. In this dimness which wouldn't be called night anymore but still isn't morning, the dreams' places and scenes, as if gradually rewinding a cassette tape, begin to emerge.] [6]


In the first half of the novel, Sakai asks a friend of his at the Bureau of Port and Harbor to help let him tour the landfill. He succeeds. Later, at the landfill, he sees a sea of plastic trash bags floating, and as he keeps looking, he sees discarded things ("handbags with broken straps, wire meshes for grilling fish gone completely rusted and tattered, nude photos from a weekly[...]" [紐の切れたハンドバッグ、錆びてぼろぼろになった魚焼きの金網、週刊誌のヌード写真]) behind the open mouths of some of those trash bags. Sakai reflects on how he can imagine stories within these objects (the prose says それぞれの物語を語っているのだった [[...]was telling each's own story.])[[7]] Then, Hino writes a Great Passage:

むせ返るような思いがけない気分だった。壊れることで、片割れだけになることで、棄てられることで、物が生き返っていた。一面見渡す限りの廃物の散乱はエントロピーの法則の勝利のしるしのはずだ。にもかかわらず、廃物のひとつひとつが無でも衰退でもない逆の濃く強烈な何ものかをつくり出していた。都市化、高層化が進むにつれて人影が次第に街路から消えてからっぽになり、空気が希薄になってゆく東京の隠された内臓が、思いきり光の下にぶちまけられているのを見る思いだった。

[It was an unexpected feeling, one that makes somebody choke as if on hot air. In breaking, in being down to a single fragment, in having been thrown away, these things were coming back to life. This littering of waste, which reached as far as he could see, should have been a sign that entropy had won out. But still, these pieces of trash, each one of them, emanated something that was neither nothingness nor decay, something that was saturated, intense. It felt like witnessing urbanizing Tōkyo with its skyscrapers, streets emptied of people's shadows, and thinning atmosphere, having spilt its innards beneath the light.][8]


As I am a visual learner, I'd like to share that I see Hino's lighting toward the innards trasmuted to a ぬめり on the surface of them (numérí, or sliminess—though I think the onomatopoeia with the nasal (mucous?) consonants adds to it), the likes of which you can kind of see in the graphics of slither.io, a multiplayer online video game (see image); you don't see where the light comes from, but you see what the light does to the thing. Taken at reverse, this is to say that when given only a surface and its patterns of sliminess, the interactions of light at a distance from the surface can be figured out. Could this be similar to how if Lyotard's 'condensation' (I am unsure whether this term could be used here) of the 3-D seen into the 2-D read is true, it might be flipped to mean the extrapolation of a 3-D seen from a 2-D read?


The way Hino calls the mannequin a "half-organism" puts the mannequin, a biological surface ("only its shape" [形だけ]—2-D?), in suspension between being a full organism or not an organism at all. Even more accurately, the biological surface is captured at a moment during its transition either from non-organism to full organism or in the other direction. Still the more accurately, the biological surface is examined for the possibility of being possessed by an organism, which it either projects inwards, in which case this is a 'soul', or projects outwards, in which case this is an 'energy'. Hino phrases this contingency in two ways (one highlighting the failure of the biological surface's aspirations, the other highlighting the success of loss on the biological surface), making it impossible for us to decide between "inwards" and "outwards" projection. This impossibility is a way of reading how Lyotard refuses to clarify whether the vertical 'seen' moves above or below the horizontal 'read'.


The passage under Contention 2 captures the same texture I experience when I slowly wake. Hino's simile between the cassette tape and the dream memory is deceptively simple, but when the cassette tape is considered for its tactility (which does not have any automatic associations with the tactility of memory) on top of its function (which already refers to storing memory), the particular mouthfeel Hino is getting at begins to luminesce.


Let's take the second half of Contention 2:

which had itself been emerging in this direction from the afterimage of a dream

The following question would be: "How, then, was it emerging in this direction from the afterimage of a dream?" Or: "What exactly, then, was emerging in this direction from the afterimage of a dream?" The answer: things that neither are supposed to emerge nor are supposed have this liquid motion in the first place. Then, the next question: "Why is it important that it emerges from the afterimage of a dream?"


A detour: the passage under Contention 1 is far from the first time in the novel mannequins are mentioned; for example, the first time Sakai encounters them is when, while walking in the city, he sees in a (shop) window an in-progress scene with eerily realistic mannequins, which he then contrasts with "those modern mannequins which are so smoothened that they are but art objects" [この頃流行の妙につるつるとオブジェ化されたマネキン] [9]. As is already evident to us from how the landfill is written (Sakai thinks he sees a mannequin in the landfill, but without any certainty; he doubts his sanity), Hino is a master at assigning imagined touch-sensations to things seen and at assembling those touch-sensations…

A word on assembly: Gilles Deleuze and Félix Guattari are famous for the unofficial putting-into-motion of the term assemblage, but their stance on the term is not to define it unitarily. In a 1980 interview with Catherine Clément, Deleuze says:

In assemblages you find states of things, bodies, various combinations of bodies, hodgepodges; but you also find utterances, modes of expression, and whole regimes of signs. The relations between the two are pretty complex. For example, a society is defined not by productive forces and ideology, but by "hodgepodges" and "verdicts." Hodgepodges are combinations of interpenetrating bodies. These combinations are well-known and accepted (incest, for example, is a forbidden combination). Verdicts are collective utterances, that is, instantaneous and incorporeal transformations which have currency in a society (for example, "from now on you are no longer a child" ...)[10]

Instead of the conceptual term 'assemblage', it might be more appropriate to say that Hino's prose pertains to the textural and processual term hodgepodge. Of course, that Sakai begins to feel an ineffable churning beneath the mannequins is still fascinating, but the technicity of Hino's prose is felt at the level of rebuilding that churning (translating it) on the page. Similarly, the way Deleuze thinks about assemblage in the passage above allows, in addition to an appreciation of being able to find different things in assemblages at large, a philological appreciation of how those things may slosh against each other (hodgepodges: the sounds they make, the chemical children born, the pain felt and diagnosed, etc.) within the assemblage.

…at hodgepodging the excrements of his own environment to make them look all the more alien and all the more abject. Often, dreams are helpless experiences when taken from the agentival standpoint of somebody in their waking state. The alienation and abjection Sakai goes through before the endless sea of trash are also helpless experiences; rereading Contention 1,

Specifically, we are led to imagine a cognition—is it ours?—growing spatially

it seems important that we are led by something alien to us, and that the spatial growth is seen in our mind's eye without necessarily giving us license to say 'we' were the ones that imagined it.


This alienation is only effective when something nauseating (i.e. something that isn't supposed to move as much as it does) floods back into the gap that was left. Taeko Ōnuki, in singing the line "A flood of people going nowhere" (over lurid synths that seem to do the same but also suggest the inside of a spaceship, plus a cover with a circular window that I'd thought was a washing machine—don't you think washing machines harness the power of the forbidden flood, the deluge that doesn't make sense?), attributes liquid motion to things motionless by nature, making those things cross a point where they can no longer return to some safe, has-been identity. Could it be that the afterimage of a dream is the substratum for not so much a 'place' as an 'atopia'—a lack-of-place; a lack-of-place-to-return-to? And could it be that our magical spatializations grow more ineffably and therefore less restrainedly (less 'repressedly' in Freudian terminology?) from an atopia than it does from a place?


I'll pose a final back-and-forth to not quite close this section:


Frédéric Neyrat, in his book (fittingly for us!) named Atopias, poses the question(s)

"Existence is, in actuality, the dismissal of being. How, then, to speak of it? If existence dismisses being, does this not simultaneously invalidate all thought? Must we invoke some mysticism of existence and affirm that we cannot speak of existence at all, except to betray its meaning?"[11]

I think he poses a partial answer to his own question when he writes a few pages later:

"From the corporeal absence of the common, a coalition is woven in all material forms, some crystallized by loves, wars, chatter, writings, magazines, assemblies, collectives-but always of the One un-filled several times and in all ways, letting the wild depths that give the coalition its sovereignty emerge."[12]


C

Contention 3: The dreamlike figuration of an 'afterimage' is a tool to represent the insecurity of a narrative standpoint. Thus, it can be very potently (though it does not need to be) used as a tool for an insecure patriarchy like Sakai's to assert itself.


When, much later in his losing sanity, Sakai canoes to a small, uninhabited island in Tokyo bay with the motorbike woman and the child, he is startled at the gooeyness of the island's vegetation; Hino remarks

何かが確実に溶けていた。オートバイで１３号地をぶっ飛ばしていた時に一瞬感じかけたふしぎな感覚に近い。外からしみこんでくるものと自分の一番奥の方から滲み出てくるものとが溶け合う感覚。それが同じものだという透きとおるような予感。どんなに酒に酔ったときよりも体の芯まで酔いきった感じ。しかも不快な嘔気はなく、しびれるようにうねりがこみ上げてくる。少年の持つ懐中電灯の光の中で、女の腰が茂みにこもる闇とともにゆらめいている。

[Something was definitely melting. The same weird feeling that was felt when they {*the motorbike woman, earlier in the novel, invites Sakai on a bike ride when she finds Sakai gazing at the bay} had been cruising in District 13 on a motorbike. The feeling where the stuff that drips in from the outside and the stuff that soaks out from the inside melt into each other. The prismatic presentiment that this stuff and that stuff make up the same thing. The kind of drunkenness that hits the core of the body more purely than any intoxication could. And without involving any nausea either, a bristling undulation rises. The boy holds up a flashlight and it points to the woman's hip shimmering, one with the darkness.][13]


A lot of Hino's descriptions and characterization objectify women by anchoring them to his background textures; the way he writes the texture itself is undoubtedly beautiful, but it seems entirely unnecessary to encode the motorcycle woman in the same language—and to make it worse, without any of her input. From another angle: it's not bad that someone has shimmering hips (it's cool, it's a superpower!), but it is bad that the act 'shimmering hips' is divested of any agency to become the target for a fetishistic desire ('I, a man, am clearly making up a woman with 'shimmering hips' as another tourist attraction in the construction of the world around me'). At the same time, it is also useful to investigate what exactly compels Hino to write women as part, and not outside, the generative geographies of dreamlikeness. Now, it is helpful to use an analogy.


Serenity Young, in her article "Buddhist Dream Experience: The Role of Interpretation, Ritual, and Gender", writes about "the need for a female guide through the dream realm that hovers between life and death" in referencing South Asian practices of using mother's milk as an eye ointment to boost good dreams, and continues (her emphasis) that "it is women who successfully traverse this realm for others biologically and, by extension, spiritually."[14] The "others" in "for others" refers to men. Men are duplicitous in their compulsion to admit women's equal and/or superior powers in accessing certain dream realms without allowing them any substantial voice in relating the experiences they have in those very dream realms. Sakai, at least, as an unreflexive actor within the narrative, is guilty of this duplicitousness. The first line of the novel illuminates this.


意識が変わり始めるとき（良く変わることも悪く変わることもある）、それに対応する何らかの出来事が必ず起こるものだ。ほとんどの場合、まずほんの偶然の、取るに足らないような形で。

[When consciousness starts to change (for better or for worse—both happen), an event outside the self happens correspondingly. Most times, in a manner that's just coincidental, almost not even worth mentioning.][15]


What about Hino's part in all of this? Is this a lower-order question of whether Hino is being gratuitous with his descriptions, or is it something higher-order, something that likely indicts Hino for perniciousness? My answer is the latter. It is not as important whether the 'event' [出来事] points precisely or in actuality to the woman's sudden appearance in Sakai's life. The statement's responsibility lies instead in the fact that, being at the very beginning of the novel, it alters readers' mindsets to consider, even for a moment, all things that happen to Sakai as metonymies for the broader category of 'event that happens to a (male) protagonist'; Hino starts us off with a pill that makes us think the possibility of 'a' woman (the motorcycle woman isn't even referred to by name—that is another big criticism I could leverage) as something that 'happens' to 'the' man as a plausibility, when such a possibility should not really be entertained in the first place. Furthermore, if to Hino the woman is an event, she is also a coincidental one, one that almost isn't worth mentioning, and most egregiously, one that responds to changes in the man's consciousness. Sure, the event could be a noetic experience that shimmers beautifully, but I'll make the same point as Young in saying that what matters is that the 'female guide', only temporarily appreciated, is eventually overwritten by the 'male dream-interpreter'.


It's hard for me to decide whether I have any case for detaching the beautiful prose Hino writes from the unalterable fact that he derives a non-insignificant part of his satisfaction from writing women as mise-en-scène. A way to clarify this question could be to investigate the mechanics of men using 'artistic inspiration' as an excuse to write women as if they were 'art objects' for men, but I don't yet feel equipped to answer it in a way that constructively adds anything to the conversation. My intuition tells me that in addition to studies on the dreamlikeness of prose by people like Hino, there should be many other studies focusing on the dreamlikeness of prose by people that outweigh Hino's mistakes and make it clear that dreamlikeness itself need not be the reason for the proliferation of patriarchal hierarchization. However, my desire to read the latter kind of prose also must be because I have not experienced dreams outside of my own patriarchal prejudices… I think the move is to pass this conversation to someone who is more qualified…
