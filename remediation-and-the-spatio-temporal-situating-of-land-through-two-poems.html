Remediation and the Spatio-Temporal Situating of Land Through Two Poets: Edward Kamau Brathwaite and Kilku Warak'a

Tai Nakamura

8 November 2026

The exposition of Frantz Fanon's essay "First Truths on the Colonial Problem" goes:


The twentieth century, when the future looks back on it, will not only be remembered as the era of atomic discoveries and interplanetary explorations. The second upheaval of this period, unquestionably, is the conquest by the peoples of the lands that belong to them. [1]


What is the difference between these two upheavals? The first upheaval, the "discoveries and [...] explorations," which are accomplished by people or entities Fanon does not name, could be portrayed as a "conquest" of the "atomic" or the "interplanetary." Potentially, the first upheaval is just one example of the second upheaval. We cannot say the upheavals inevitably differ solely based on the details we have about the 'discoverer'/'conquerer' (agent) or about the 'discovered/conquered' (patient). If the second upheaval is to be a marked contrast from the first upheaval, the contrast must pivot on a reading of the phrase "the lands that belong to them": what legitimizes 'their' claim to 'the lands,' who 'they' must be to claim 'the lands,' whether 'the lands' can be known as a referent of 'their' conquering.


Then, a second set of questions becomes relevant. First, what assumptions must exist for the pairing of the terms "lands" and "belong" to make sense? Second, what happens when those assumptions are decontextualized or misread… will this pairing, "lands" and "belong," feel unfamiliar or illegible? The third question is a rephrasing of the second: can we, and how can we, defamiliarize or appropriate the pairing so that those assumptions can be challenged?


I will first clarify that considering the language around land, around ownership, and around land in conjunction with ownership does not guarantee any repair or redress, insofar as those terms involve a tangible correction or a tangible piecing together. However, thinking about language is firmly within the scope of remedial practice. I see remedy as a form of self-care that helps people who, in repairing and redressing, must externalize a lot of their energy.


One example in short: a certain subset of United States liberals likes to ridicule land acknowledgement. The problem is their misattribution of a certain kind of telos to land acknowledgement. To these people, the language of acknowledgement is entirely incommensurable with this ostensible telos. This unrigorous telos is to enforce the transfer of ownership of this land 'back' to whoever the addressees of the acknowledgement are. An isolated land acknowledgement (like the plaques on Columbia's campus) is made to lack a remedial element. Such a remedial element would contextualize the language of a land acknowledgement with the sequence of exact concrete tasks it is supposed to evoke. It would not jump ridiculously to a vague, unintelligible notion of 'ideal' or 'completed' repair and redress still dominated by the current havers of the land. This disjunction illuminates how imagining land (re-)ownership hinges on how well-defined, and in which direction, the linguistic expression of return projects itself.


With remedy in mind, then, let's read how land, its (re-)ownership, and its language of return manifest in two poets' work. The Barbadian poet Edward Kamau Brathwaite's (1930-2020) trilogy The Arrivants (1973), to give a certainly oversimplified introduction, flexibly indexes memories of the continent Africa as comingling with fragments of Caribbean devotion and speech which often refuse accumulation or cataloguing but are themselves very capable of it. The Peruvian poet Kilku Warak'a's (1909-1984, other name Andrés Alencastre) poetry collection Taki Parwa (1952), some of which was translated into Spanish by Odi Gonzales in 1999, is written entirely in Runasimi and consists of odes to the types of images and loves whose uniqueness Kilku Warak'a defines by alternately referencing landmarks (like Cochabamba, Puno, or Macu Pikcu) and tropes of people (like ususi (daughter), mama (mother), or wakcha (the orphaned)). I will now transcribe an excerpt each from Brathwaite's and Kilku Warak'a's works:

Both works—Brathwaite at a double stroke toward Africa and 'his' Caribbean, and Kilku Warak'a toward a Peruvian Runasimi-speaking 'periphery' away from the metropole of Lima—reach at a certain valence of land.


Kilku Warak'a characterizes his reaching with the metaphor that the land is a mother. In poems such as "Mamaquca,"[3] the title assists the metaphor: mama quca, or 'mother-lake' taken lexeme-by-lexeme, is a common Runasimi word to describe "ocean"—a body of water, like the poem's subject Titiqaqa, of massive proportions. However, in "Llaqtalláy" (which roughly glosses as "My Only Town," where 'only' is almost a vocative), the punchline in the sixth and last stanza is that the town indeed turns out to be the speaker's mother[4].


That the land or landmark Kilku Warak'a refers to is a mother is not important unless it is scaffolded by a conceptual metaphor[5] of return interpretable as a type of non-immediate motion. This he creates by drawing an analogy between two contrasts, one in the first stanza and the other in the third. The first stanza counterpoints the speaker's rememberance of the town (line 1: "Yupa munasqay llaqtallay llaqta") with an entreaty to themself not to forget the town (line 2: "qunqaykimancu").[6: READ NOTE] The third stanza counterpoints a distancing from the town (line 9: "Sapa astawan karuncaqtiymi") with the vanishing point for a return (line 10: "kuticimuwan") that comes as a result of this distancing.[7: READ NOTE] Importantly, the gradient from the presence to the absence of the memory is mapped against a spatiotemporal log that determines what past trends in distance call for a future of return.


The second stanza's "aswan aswantan qanta munayki (line 7: More and more I long for you)" is a repetitive thickening caress that maps to the third stanza's "usqhay usqhayta, sunquypi kawsaq / cay rick'ayllayki (lines 11-12: Quick, quickly, the life within my heart / this is you, I wake you)" repetitive quickening caress. As I read in note 6 and note 7, because return is tangible while the memory of departure/distancing can only be conceptualized as a 'thing,' and because the relationship between return (-mu- infix) and departure/distancing (-pu- infix) itself is tenuous, it makes sense that Kilku Warak'a thinks of desire (the desire for return that precedes the actual landgazing of a return ...

as accumulating in abstract space, whereas the 'awakening' of the town—temporally tangible—is a matter of quickness.


The pared-down notion of 'return,' which collapses its reach in time to a sloganistic "acknowledgement" of space, is challenged when Kilku Warak'a demonstrates how the concreteness of spatial claiming is cast from the same mould as the mechanism by which temporal 'return' exposes and compromises the incompleteness of its past of estrangement. The excerpt I chose from Brathwaite's "Homecoming" is emblematic of The Arrivants as a whole in that it recognizes this concreteness-estrangement connection and attempts to identify (then to remediate) its splintered edges by naming its iconography either as a recentering ('reclaiming'?) of present territorial possession or as debris from a forgotten departure.


In the listed passage, Brathwaite's repetitive caress is the naming of "Shackles, shackles, shackles" (line 7) in its third stanza. Without a bifurcation like Kilku Warak'a's (aswanta versus osqhayta), the stress of both timeless material accumulation and spaceless visualization of return rests on the shackles.


On the outside of these shackles, the speaker observes the fragmentariness of their own devotion (lines 4-6: "clinks / of dew in the grass / is the nearest we will get to god.") in contrast to the astrologically, internally sutured 'property' of the master (a white plantation owner, or somebody in a similar position of oppressive power). Would devotion (regardless of the situation of knowing in which it is attempted) be fragmentary and property (regardless of the type of fist) be a self-connected assumption of subjecthood?


On the inside of the shackles, Brathwaite schemes an answer: after defining "shackles" by writing that they "are my peace, are my home, / are my evening song" (lines 8-9), he hones in on the word "home," metastasizing the "us" of the poem to the standpoint of the fevered cat (line 12) from which there is "no name / to call us home" (lines 14-15, empahsis added). That the fevered cat must be called home is the same requirement (see note 7), the same unanthropologizable divine implied by the factive/causative infixes -cha- and -ci- when Kilku Warak'a's speaker becomes distant and is compelled to (re)turn. Only by triangulating rabidity—a somebody's qunqasqan—can "we" draw karu and kuti by the same stroke, a disembodied stroke at that.[8]


Now I present to you a possible key to the lock. Elsewhere in The Arrivants—at the end of a subsection titled "Limits" and right before a subsection titled "Return"—Brathwaite performs a gesture of opening from, and comments on the semantics of, a 'limit' toward 'return' ... and how striking is it that "The White River" is the title of this reaching, a Malian and Arkansan Vaitarani.


(click to pop out) Part '2' of "The White River,"[9] The Arrivants, 122


This was at last the last;

this was the limit of motion;

voyages ended;

time stopped where its movement began;


horizons returned inaccessible.

Here was at last the limit;

the minutes of pebbles drop-

ping into the hourless pool.


Hands reached into water;

gods nudged us like fish;

black bottomless whales that we worshipped.

O new world of want, who will build the new ways,


the new ships?



Let me first collect myself from the dazzlement I experienced amidst the density of implorings and imaginings in this excerpt. Where to begin? I think that the enjambment on "drop- / ping" (lines 7-8), the only enjambment of this kind in the excerpt, is the pivot. Simultaneously coinhabiting this pivot are the onomatopoeic sound of dropping ("ping"), the noun-ification of the gerund dropping ("drop"), and the silhouette of the severed word [10] on the verge of a drop to the next line. Also, though, there is a named something that 'drops' other than the word itself: that something is "the minutes of pebbles" (line 7). Here, Brathwaite is witness to a 刹那/क्षण[11] within which a fragment of time and a fragment of spatial matter achieve absolute inseparability.


Brathwaite prepares us for this conjuncture by repeatedly hinting at a "limit," a conjectural "horizons returned inaccessible" (line 5) in which "voyages ended" (line 3) and the water—bloddy Atlantic expanses and Caribbean expanses in repose before viewership—became "hourless" (line 8). The past tense, "voyages ended," ascribes to the speaker of the poem a tone of ultimacy, as if the speaker is already at the destination ... even if (even though) they aren't. However, the "hourless pool" only loses its hour-ness when time "stop[s] where its movement began" (line 4), which is to say that any attempt to trace where time henceforth voyaged would also have to contend with a point of origin that does not move, cannot be defrosted. Now recall that in "Llaqtalláy," the primordial reference point of rememberance and original distancing cannot be promoted to verb, and that what is verb is a scrappy attempt not to forget or not to lose the desire to turn back.


Whereever and whenever the type of "conquest" spearheaded by the scientific aesthetic, atoms and outer space and synaptic flashes, contents itself with having discovered thing X or thing Y, "the conquest by the peoples of the lands that belong to them"[1] must contend with the slippage of an actionable conquest into a noun-ified 'accomplishment' for which the history of return (i.e. the initial estrangement and the desire to return) then becomes unreferenceable. To consider the example Adom Getachew raises in political scientific discourse, this noun-ification generated nation-states from the verb-like contingency during the self-assertion of anticolonial nationalism and the sense of disappointment or stagnation that came along when those nation-states took root.[12]


Remediation must be a reminder that the memory of the contingencies (conjectures) of worldmaking is not automatically excluded from the conjuncture of time and space which 'fulfills' the nation-state or the voyage or the return to the 'mother'land. Brathwaite and Kilku Warak'a's reminders are effective because both concentrate on exclusivity: the conclusion of "Homecoming" (lines 16-18 in the excerpt: "to bring us soft- / ly past these bars to miracle, to god, / to unexpected lover.") is distinctly unclear on the degree of universality of the "lover" who is the "god" to the "us" (or, the "god" who is the "lover" to (each of/all of) the "us"), and Kilku Warak'a in "Llaqtalláy" connects the speaker's "llaqtalláy" (title, line 1, and line 8) to the verbs "hanpullasaqmi" (line 3) and "rick'ayllayki" (line 12) via the infix -lla-.[13]


The question of ocularcentrism I raised earlier seems to refer this particularity to how (to take one example) "White River" opens with "From the Akuapim ridge un- / rolled a new land"[14]; it also refers this particularity to the reason why Kilku Warak'a promises to 'wake' (rick'ayllayki) the town within his heart as a preventative measure against forgetting. The exclusivity of the speaker's devotion is evidence for the sometimes one-way, sometimes two-way gaze that happens between the land and the speaker. The land from the bared and opaque eye of the speaker is an image waken, or an unrollable surface, or—flooding the human subject—"ris[ing] slowly / fed by the ringed sun and the distant Amazon: / leaves, seed, silt, feathers, / broken wings, hooks, clutching eyes, / bugs, green-backed bats, leeches"[15]: there is a conflict between the reclaimant, who can only gaze, and the voluptuous land, who overfills the frame for any one gaze.


An unconquerable land repudiates Fanon's "conquest" (a type of gaze, possibly) at face value. However, seeing that neither Brathwaite nor Kilku Warak'a shys away from a language of gaze and of first-person return, of the land as an object for action or an aphrodisiac 'serving' the speaker (particularly clear in Kilku Warak'a's distance-desire dyad), of gaze and first-person return as hermeneutic highlighters, I could concede that Fanon was attempting a workable definition of a dialogue between the speaker and the land that, when opened, is much more thickly entangled than the garden-variety flat-space/flat-time (re-)ownership of land. We should read Fanon's "conquest" as another hermeneutic highlighter. I can clarify the questions I asked in the opening:

  • "what legitimizes 'their' claim to 'the lands'" becomes "how legitimation summons history as a noun and action as a verb"
  • "who 'they' must be to claim 'the lands'" becomes "how 'they' (the speaker) performs the silent transition from noun-history to verb-action through their language"
  • "whether 'the lands' can be known as a referent of 'their' conquering" becomes "what kinds of ways of compiling/embodying/(other verb) history allow or disallow the land to speak back or to claim aprioriness."


After revisiting my initial exploration of remediation, I want to conclude that locating and rigorously defining the type of space and the type of time of land (as we see it and as it may speak for itself) is as important as locating and rigourously defining the type of space and the type of time the "people of the lands" assume subjecthood in... if any (re-)joindering between the "people" and the "lands" is to be had. The imagination of land does change after "conquest," just as the imagination of the body does change after 'self-conquest.' I'm hoping that my readings of Edward Kamau Brathwaite's and Kilku Warak'a's poetry opens up a question of 'who' acts in time and 'what' is placed in abstract space when a human thinks of land, its past, present, and future, its closeness, farness, or unreachability.

[5]: Here, I use 'conceptual metaphor' in George Lakoff's sense. Lakoff, "How Metaphor Structures Dreams: The Theory of Conceptual Metaphor Applied to Dream Analysis," in Dreaming 3, no.2 (1993): 77-98.

[6]: Yupay is the verb that means "to remember"; "Qunqay" (or qonqay, as in qonqaylla, which means "suddenly") is the verb that means "to forget." Notably, the verb is turned into an adjectival descriptor for the town (yupa, or "remembered") whilst the verbal element of qunqay is preserved (qunqaykimanchu, or "May I not forget you"). Forgetting is a real, actionable possibility which must be remediated, whereas remembrance and memory can only be seen without a time of action; I discuss this double temporality further in note 7.

[7]: Karu is an adjective meaning "distant." The infix -cha- is inserted whenever the subject is 'turning into' said adjective (e.g. karuchay means "to turn distant"). Kuti is often translated as "turn"; the infix -ci- means "to make do [verb]"; the translocative infix -mu- refers to an action that begins or happens somewhere else but ends or ends-up-being-described at the location of the speaker; the infix -wa- denotes an action that somebody else does to the first-person speaker. In summary, kuticimuwan can be translated as "make me return from there to here." Two notable things:

  • Kilku Warak'a does not use the infix -mpu- (the addition of -mu- to the infix -pu-, which conversely refers to an action which starts 'here' and ends 'there'), which would contextualize that the action happens from 'there' to 'here' only because previously, there had been an analogous departure from 'here' to 'there'; for instance, "kutimpuy" would mean "to return from a place toward which one had once departed." Kilku Warak'a's purview only accommodates the motion from 'there' to 'here'; we must think about what caused the initial motion from 'here' to 'there'—the 'history' or 'context' of the return—to now have become unintelligible.
  • The verb kutiy can also be used to describe a physical turning over (like turning over a piece of paper). Though I translated this passage as "harder you pull me to return,"" the word "kuticimuwan" could mean "make me turn (my head, my body) from there to here." We must read turn and return as leaking into each other.

[8]: Is this the unanthropologizable divine what Ranajit Guha criticizes history writing for reducing to "an unadulterated secularism" when 'analyzing the psychology' of the Santal rebellion? Guha, "The Prose of Counter-Insurgency," 37.
